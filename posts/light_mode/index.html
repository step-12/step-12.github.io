    In Defense of Light Mode

    September 18, 2026

    I have reached an age at which I have become mildly irritated by the color scheme of my computer.

    This is not, I should stress, an important problem.

    There are wars, famines, elections, climate change, and JavaScript frameworks that have somehow acquired build systems larger than operating systems. And yet here I am, wanting to talk about light mode.

    Because apparently I am now required to defend myself for wanting a white background behind my text.

    I like light mode.

    There. I said it.

    I like black—or nearly black—letters on a light background. I like reasonably large type. I like a room with some actual illumination in it. I like being able to read things without first turning the entire interface into a cave and then discovering that the pale gray text is apparently supposed to be visible.

    And I am increasingly suspicious of the proposition that dark mode is somehow better for your eyes.

    It may be better for your eyes.

    It is not necessarily better for my eyes.

    Those are rather different statements.

    The Great Dark Mode Revelation#

    Somewhere along the way, dark mode acquired a remarkable collection of supposed virtues.

    It is easier on the eyes.

    It reduces eye strain.

    It protects your eyesight.

    It is better for your long-term vision.

    It is somehow more natural.

    Perhaps it also makes your coffee taste better.

    Some of these claims have a kernel of truth. Some are reasonable hypotheses. Some have achieved the peculiar status of things that are repeated so often that nobody remembers whether there was ever any evidence for them.

    The problem is the phrase:

    “better for your eyes.”

    Better in what sense?

    Better for whom?

    Under what lighting conditions?

    For what task?

    For how long?

    At what brightness?

    With what font size?

    And whose eyes?

    Because eyes are not identical pieces of consumer electronics.

    What Your Eyes Are Actually Looking At#

    Let’s start with something rather obvious.

    A computer screen emits light.

    A white portion of a screen emits more light than a black portion of a screen.

    So, yes, if I turn my entire screen white, I am putting considerably more light into the room—and into my eyes—than if I turn it black.

    But that does not mean that white pixels are slowly cooking my retinas.

    This is an important distinction.

    There is a substantial difference between:

    “This is bright and uncomfortable.”

    and

    “This is damaging my eyes.”

    Ordinary computer displays are not remotely equivalent to staring at the sun. The evidence does not show that normal use of a computer display in either light or dark mode causes progressive retinal damage simply because one uses more white pixels.

    There is plenty of evidence that prolonged computer use can produce digital eye strain: tired eyes, dryness, burning, headaches, blurred vision, difficulty focusing and general ocular discomfort.

    But eye strain is not the same thing as eye damage.

    Your eyes can get tired without being injured.

    Your legs can get tired from walking without concluding that walking is destroying your legs.

    The same basic distinction applies here.

    Then Why Does Brightness Matter?#

    Because comfort is real.

    The fact that something isn’t damaging your retina doesn’t mean it is pleasant to stare at for eight hours.

    The amount of light coming from the display matters. So does the amount of light in the room.

    Imagine two situations.

    Situation 1#

    You are sitting in a reasonably well-lit office.

    Your monitor is moderately bright.

    The text is large.

    The contrast is good.

    Nothing is reflecting directly into the screen.

    Lovely.

    Situation 2#

    It is midnight.

    The room is almost completely dark.

    You have a gigantic white browser window open at maximum brightness.

    Your pupils are adapted to darkness and there is now a luminous rectangle several hundred times brighter than its surroundings sitting in front of you.

    This is probably not going to feel wonderful.

    The important variable isn’t simply:

    WHITE BAD.

    It is the relationship between the display and the surrounding visual environment.

    The screen should generally be comfortable relative to the room you are in.

    This is one reason the “dark mode is healthier” argument is too simplistic. Dark mode may be extremely pleasant in a dark environment because it reduces the amount of light coming from the display.

    That doesn’t establish that dark mode is intrinsically healthier.

    It establishes that not staring at a blazing rectangle in a dark room can be pleasant.

    A surprisingly modest achievement.

    There Is Also the Matter of Contrast#

    Here is where things become particularly interesting.

    The human visual system does not merely care about how much light there is.

    It cares about differences in luminance.

    Text has to be distinguished from its background.

    And as we get older, our ability to distinguish subtle differences in contrast generally declines.

    This is one reason that:

    dark gray text on slightly lighter gray

    can be lovely to a designer and absolutely infuriating to somebody trying to read the documentation.

    At some point, “subtle” becomes “where the hell is the text?”

    High contrast can make text easier to distinguish.

    And there is nothing inherently virtuous about low contrast.

    A large field of light background with dark characters provides a very strong, familiar contrast relationship.

    That is one reason books have historically looked roughly like this:

    black marks on something light.

    It is not because Gutenberg had failed to discover dark mode.

    And Then You Turn 45#

    Here is where I have to confess that this entire argument has become considerably more personal.

    I recently turned 45.

    At 25, I could apparently read anything.

    A line of code in microscopic gray type?

    Fine.

    A terminal with tiny characters?

    Fine.

    A badly designed website with 13 different shades of gray?

    Apparently I was invincible.

    At 45, I would like the text to be larger.

    I would like the contrast to be better.

    I would like the room to contain light.

    And I would like whoever designed the interface to stop whispering the text at me.

    This isn’t entirely psychological.

    The eye changes with age.

    One of the most familiar changes is presbyopia. The lens of the eye gradually becomes less capable of changing shape to focus on nearby objects. This is why people who have spent their entire lives being able to read at whatever distance they pleased eventually discover that their phone has somehow become farther away every year.

    The phone hasn’t moved.

    Your arm has merely become part of the optical apparatus.

    The lens also becomes less transparent with age, and there is increasing scattering of light within the eye. The pupil tends to become smaller, too. Less light reaches the retina.

    At the same time, contrast sensitivity can decline.

    So the visual system of a 45-year-old is not necessarily experiencing the same display in the same way as that of a 20-year-old.

    This does not mean:

    “Everyone over 40 should use light mode.”

    It means something much more boring and useful:

    Age changes the visual conditions under which an individual finds things comfortable and legible.

    And that is precisely why universal proclamations about display modes are suspicious.

    More Light Is Not Automatically Worse#

    There is another amusing complication.

    Because the aging eye receives less light, adequate illumination can actually become more useful with age.

    That doesn’t mean “make everything as bright as possible.”

    It means that the simplistic equation:

    less light = healthier eyes

    doesn’t work.

    For some visual tasks, more illumination improves visual performance.

    A well-lit environment can help an older person see.

    A sufficiently bright display can make text easier to distinguish.

    Higher contrast can make reading easier.

    Larger text reduces the demand on visual resolution and focusing.

    And suddenly we have arrived at something rather less fashionable than “dark mode is healthier”:

    Make things easy to see.

    What a radical design philosophy.

    The Curious Rise of Dark Mode#

    Now we arrive at the part of this story that I find rather amusing.

    Dark mode did not descend from Mount Olympus bearing a tablet on which the ophthalmologists had written:

    Thou shalt henceforth use dark backgrounds.

    It became popular for a number of perfectly ordinary technological and aesthetic reasons.

    One of those reasons was OLED.

    An OLED display does not work like an old LCD display with a single backlight shining through the entire panel. Individual pixels emit their own light. A genuinely black pixel can therefore be turned essentially off.

    And this has an obvious consequence:

    Black pixels can save power on OLED displays.

    If half your screen is black, the display doesn’t have to illuminate those pixels.

    On a phone, where battery life is precious, this is not a trivial consideration.

    Dark interfaces therefore had a very practical technological justification, particularly as OLED displays became widespread.

    Then came the aesthetic justification.

    Dark interfaces look futuristic.

    Dark interfaces look technical.

    Dark interfaces look like you are operating a nuclear submarine.

    Dark interfaces make ordinary software appear considerably more important than it probably is.

    Developers, naturally, loved this.

    Terminal windows had already given us decades of black backgrounds. Code editors embraced them. Then operating systems did. Then browsers did. Then websites did. Then every application on Earth acquired a little crescent-moon icon.

    And eventually we arrived at the modern situation in which an application with seventeen settings has a menu containing:

    Light Dark System Dim Midnight AMOLED Solarized High Contrast Dracula Gruvbox Something called “OLED Black”

    At this point, we should perhaps admit that some of this is customisation for its own sake.

    Dark Mode Became a Feature#

    This is another important part of the story.

    Software increasingly stopped telling us how it wanted to look.

    Instead, it began asking us.

    What theme?

    What font?

    What accent colour?

    Rounded corners?

    Sharp corners?

    Compact?

    Comfortable?

    Dense?

    Spacious?

    Light?

    Dark?

    System?

    High contrast?

    Custom?

    There is something wonderful about giving people control over their tools.

    But customisability also creates a peculiar cultural phenomenon:

    If there is a setting, people begin to assume that one of the settings must be objectively superior.

    Dark mode is a particularly good example.

    It went from:

    “Here is an alternative visual presentation.”

    to:

    “Here is the presentation you should probably be using.”

    And then, inevitably:

    “Here is why the presentation you should probably be using is healthier.”

    That last step is where I become suspicious.

    A preference became a feature.

    The feature became a trend.

    The trend acquired a rationale.

    The rationale became a factoid.

    And the factoid eventually became something people confidently tell other people about their eyes.

    Dark Mode May Be Better. It May Not.#

    There is nothing wrong with the proposition that dark mode feels better for some people.

    It can reduce overall screen luminance.

    It can be particularly comfortable in low-light environments.

    It can reduce the visual impact of a large bright screen.

    Some people find it easier to look at for long periods.

    And, on OLED screens, it can reduce power consumption.

    All perfectly respectable reasons.

    But these don’t add up to:

    Dark mode is universally healthier for human vision.

    The research on visual comfort and display polarity is not nearly that tidy. Studies have found differences in legibility, visual performance and subjective comfort, but the results are not consistent enough to declare a universal winner.

    And that’s hardly surprising.

    The human eye is not a machine with a single optimum brightness setting.

    Light Mode Has a Rather Long Pedigree#

    There is also a peculiar assumption that light mode is some sort of antiquated interface convention that we have finally escaped.

    It is worth remembering what we spent several thousand years doing.

    We wrote things on light surfaces.

    Paper.

    Books.

    Manuscripts.

    Newspapers.

    Maps.

    Letters.

    The fact that we don’t traditionally print white letters on black paper isn’t proof that paper manufacturers were conspiring against OLED owners.

    Dark text on a light background has an enormous amount of history behind it because it works very well for many forms of reading.

    That doesn’t make it universally superior.

    It does, however, make the claim that light mode is somehow inherently hostile to the human visual system rather difficult to sustain.

    The Problem With “Better”#

    This is really the crux of the matter.

    Suppose I say:

    “I find light mode easier to read.”

    There isn’t much to argue with.

    That’s a statement about my experience.

    Suppose you say:

    “I find dark mode easier to read.”

    Also fine.

    Now suppose someone says:

    “Dark mode is better for your eyes.”

    We’ve changed the nature of the claim.

    We have moved from preference to medical proposition.

    And medical propositions require rather more evidence.

    If the claim is instead:

    “Dark mode can be more comfortable for some people, particularly in darker environments.”

    Fine.

    If the claim is:

    “Dark mode can reduce power consumption on OLED displays.”

    Fine.

    If the claim is:

    “Some people simply prefer it.”

    Obviously.

    If the claim is:

    “Dark mode prevents long-term damage to your eyes.”

    That is a much stronger claim, and the evidence does not justify treating it as established fact.

    So Here I Am, at 45#

    I have no desire to persuade anyone to abandon dark mode.

    Quite the contrary.

    If you like it, use it.

    If it makes your screen more comfortable, excellent.

    If it saves your phone battery, even better.

    If you simply think it looks nice, I have no objection.

    But I reserve the right to use a bright background, large letters and strong contrast without being told that I am mistreating my eyes.

    Because my eyes are 45 years old.

    They have started sending requirements.

    They would like larger type.

    They would like better illumination.

    They would like decent contrast.

    They would like fewer reflections.

    They would like me to stop pretending that tiny gray text is sophisticated.

    And, apparently, they would like light mode.

    The eyes have spoken.

    The Actual Rule#

    There isn’t a universally correct mode.

    There is only a display, an environment, a task and a pair of eyes.

    For one person:

    dark background + moderate brightness + large text

    may be ideal.

    For another:

    light background + high contrast + large text

    may be ideal.

    For somebody working at night, the first may be much more comfortable.

    For somebody reading dense text in a well-lit office, the second may be preferable.

    For somebody with aging eyes, increasing font size and ensuring adequate illumination may become increasingly important.

    And for somebody with a particular eye condition, the right answer may be something else entirely.

    So perhaps we should stop asking:

    “Which mode is better?”

    and ask:

    “Which mode lets you see comfortably?”

    That is a much less exciting question.

    It also happens to be the useful one.

    As for me, I shall continue to use light mode.

    Not because it is medically superior.

    Not because dark mode is bad.

    Not because I have discovered some secret truth about photons.

    Simply because I can see the bloody thing better.

    And at 45, that seems like a perfectly respectable criterion.
