    Logic, Math, Boolean, Sets

    Logic: The Machinery of Reasoning#

    You already know more logic than you think.

    Every time you say:

    “If it is raining, I should carry an umbrella.”

    or:

    “I will have tea if there is milk and sugar.”

    or:

    “I will go out if it is Saturday or Sunday.”

    you are using logic.

    You don’t need a computer, a formula, or a mathematics degree to use it. Logic is simply a way of describing how statements combine and how we reason from one statement to another.

    We have already covered the mechanics of Boolean logic in class. This post is a chance to step back and look at the bigger picture: what logic is, why it is useful, and why the same ideas keep appearing in apparently unrelated parts of mathematics.

    1. Logic is about true and false#

    Let’s start with some ordinary statements:

    It is raining.
The shop is open.
Today is Monday.
I have a ticket.
7 is an even number.

    Each of these can be classified as true or false.

    For example:

    "7 is an even number."

    is simply false.

    Logic gives us a way to take statements like these and combine them into larger statements.

    Suppose:

    A = It is raining.
B = I am carrying an umbrella.

    Then:

    A AND B

    means:

    It is raining and I am carrying an umbrella.

    Similarly:

    A OR B

    means:

    It is raining or I am carrying an umbrella.

    And:

    NOT A

    means:

    It is not raining.

    This is the basic idea behind Boolean logic.

    2. Why is this called Boolean logic?#

    The name comes from George Boole, a 19th-century English mathematician.

    Boole was interested in a rather ambitious question:

    Can reasoning itself be expressed using mathematics?

    In 1854, he published An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, developing a system in which statements could be manipulated using mathematical rules.

    He wasn’t thinking about Python, computers, or mobile phones. None of those things existed.

    Yet his system turned out to be extraordinarily useful for computers.

    Why?

    Because computers ultimately have to distinguish between two states. A circuit can be on or off. A switch can be open or closed. A bit can be 1 or 0.

    So we can associate:

    TRUE  ↔  1
FALSE ↔  0

    And suddenly the mathematics of reasoning becomes useful for building machines.

    This is one of the fun things about mathematics:

    You don’t always know what an idea will eventually be useful for.

    3. A tiny language for reasoning#

    The basic logical operations you have already seen give us a remarkably small language.

    Suppose:

    A = It is raining.
B = I have an umbrella.

    We can make statements such as:

    NOT A
A AND B
A OR B
A XOR B

    The first three are probably familiar.

    XOR is slightly more particular. It means one or the other, but not both.

    For example:

    A = I am having tea.
B = I am having coffee.

    Then:

    A XOR B

    means:

    I am having either tea or coffee, but not both.

    The important thing is that we can build bigger statements from these small pieces.

    For example:

    A = It is raining.
B = I have an umbrella.
C = I am going outside.

C AND (A AND B)

    means:

    I am going outside, it is raining, and I have an umbrella.

    We are taking ordinary sentences and treating them almost like building blocks.

    4. Logic is also algebra#

    This is where Boolean logic starts becoming interesting.

    In ordinary algebra, you can manipulate expressions without changing what they mean.

    For example:

    2 × (3 + 4)

    can be rewritten as:

    (2 × 3) + (2 × 4)

    Boolean logic has its own rules for doing something similar.

    One of the most famous examples is De Morgan’s theorem:

    NOT (A AND B) = (NOT A) OR (NOT B)

NOT (A OR B) = (NOT A) AND (NOT B)

    The notation may look intimidating, but the idea is quite natural.

    Imagine:

    A = It is raining.
B = It is cold.

    Consider:

    It is not true that it is raining and cold.

    For that to be true, at least one of the two things must fail:

    It is not raining or it is not cold.

    So:

    NOT (raining AND cold)

    is equivalent to:

    NOT raining OR NOT cold

    Here’s another everyday example.

    Suppose I say:

    I am not having both tea and coffee.

    That means:

    I am not having tea or I am not having coffee.

    Notice how the AND became an OR, and the NOT moved inside.

    That’s De Morgan’s theorem in ordinary language.

    5. Statements can talk about other statements#

    So far, we have mostly been combining statements.

    Formal logic goes one step further and lets us talk about relationships between statements.

    Consider:

    If it is raining, then the ground is wet.

    Let’s give the two parts names:

    A = It is raining.
B = The ground is wet.

    Then we can write:

    A → B

    which means:

    If A is true, then B is true.

    This is called a conditional.

    We use conditionals constantly in ordinary life:

    If I am hungry, I will eat.
If the shop is closed, I will come back tomorrow.
If today is Sunday, I don't have to go to work.
If a number is divisible by 4, it is even.

    The interesting thing is that once we have a conditional, we can transform it in several different ways.

    6. The family of conditional statements#

    Start with a simple statement:

    If it is raining, then the ground is wet.

    Let’s call the two parts:

    A = It is raining.
B = The ground is wet.

    Our original statement is therefore:

    A → B

    There are three particularly important ways of transforming this statement.

    6.1 The converse#

    The converse swaps the two parts:

    If the ground is wet, then it is raining.

    In symbols:

    A → B

B → A

    This sounds reasonable at first, but it is not necessarily true.

    The ground could be wet because someone watered the garden.

    So the converse is related to the original statement, but it is not generally equivalent to it.

    6.2 The inverse#

    The inverse negates both parts:

    If it is not raining, then the ground is not wet.

    In symbols:

    A → B

NOT A → NOT B

    Again, this is not necessarily true.

    It could be sunny while the garden is being watered.

    So:

    If it is not raining, the ground is not wet.

    doesn’t follow from:

    If it is raining, the ground is wet.

    6.3 The contrapositive#

    The contrapositive does two things:

    1. Swap the two parts.
    2. Negate both of them.

    Starting with:

    If it is raining, then the ground is wet.

    we get:

    If the ground is not wet, then it is not raining.

    In symbols:

    A → B

NOT B → NOT A

    And this one is special:

    A statement and its contrapositive are logically equivalent.

    If the original statement is true, its contrapositive must be true, and vice versa.

    7. The four forms side by side#

    It is useful to see the whole family together.

    Starting with:

    If it is raining, then the ground is wet.

    we get:

    FormStatementEquivalent to original?
    ConditionalIf it is raining, the ground is wet.—
    ConverseIf the ground is wet, it is raining.❌
    InverseIf it is not raining, the ground is not wet.❌
    ContrapositiveIf the ground is not wet, it is not raining.✅

    There is a useful relationship hiding here:

    Conditional  →  Contrapositive
      ↕              ↕
Converse    →    Inverse

    The conditional and contrapositive are equivalent.

    The converse and inverse are also equivalent to each other.

    But neither pair is generally equivalent to the other.

    So, if:

    A → B

    is true, we automatically know:

    NOT B → NOT A

    is true.

    But we cannot automatically conclude:

    B → A

    or:

    NOT A → NOT B

    are true.

    This distinction is worth remembering because reversing a statement is not the same thing as taking its contrapositive.

    8. A mathematical example#

    Consider:

    If a number is divisible by 4, then it is even.

    Let:

    A = The number is divisible by 4.
B = The number is even.

    The four forms are:

    Conditional

    If a number is divisible by 4, it is even.

    Converse

    If a number is even, it is divisible by 4.

    Not true. 6 is even, but it isn’t divisible by 4.

    Inverse

    If a number is not divisible by 4, it is not even.

    Also not true. 6 isn’t divisible by 4, but it is even.

    Contrapositive

    If a number is not even, it is not divisible by 4.

    True.

    Notice how useful the contrapositive can be. Sometimes proving or understanding:

    If A, then B

    is easier by thinking about:

    If NOT B, then NOT A.

    9. Logic and sets are surprisingly similar#

    Now let’s leave statements for a moment and look at sets.

    Suppose we have a group of people who like tea and a group of people who like coffee.

    A = people who like tea
B = people who like coffee

    What does the intersection mean?

    A ∩ B

    It means:

    People who like tea AND coffee.

    What does the union mean?

    A ∪ B

    It means:

    People who like tea OR coffee.

    This is not merely a convenient analogy.

    The correspondence is exact.

    For any person x:

    x ∈ A ∩ B

    means:

    x is in A AND x is in B.

    And:

    x ∈ A ∪ B

    means:

    x is in A OR x is in B.

    We can think of it like this:

    flowchart LR
    A["People who like tea"] --> I["Intersection ∩"]
    B["People who like coffee"] --> I
    I --> R["Like tea AND coffee"]

    A2["People who like tea"] --> U["Union ∪"]
    B2["People who like coffee"] --> U
    U --> R2["Like tea OR coffee"]

    There is another correspondence too.

    The complement of a set means:

    Everything that is not in the set.

    That is very similar to NOT in logic.

    So we have:

    Logic                  Sets

AND                    ∩
OR                     ∪
NOT                    Complement

    Different objects, different notation, but remarkably similar rules.

    10. The same idea keeps appearing#

    This is one of the most important things to notice about mathematics.

    Mathematics is not just a giant collection of unrelated topics.

    Very often, different mathematical systems describe the same underlying structure.

    Consider:

    Boolean logic
Set theory
Digital circuits
Programming

    They look like completely different subjects.

    But all of them can contain versions of:

    AND
OR
NOT

    For example:

    LogicSetsProgramming
    ANDIntersection ∩and
    ORUnion ∪or
    NOTComplementnot

    The symbols aren’t really the important part.

    The important thing is that the relationships between the operations are the same.

    Once you start noticing this, mathematics becomes a little less like memorising hundreds of separate rules.

    You start asking:

    “Is this actually the same idea I’ve seen somewhere else?”

    Quite often, the answer is yes.

    11. Different mathematical worlds can describe the same thing#

    Here’s an even stranger idea.

    Suppose you want to describe the number 3.

    You could simply write:

    3

    But mathematics gives us many other ways to construct objects.

    For example, we could describe numbers using sets. We could describe operations using functions. We could describe relationships using logic.

    In principle, entire branches of mathematics can be constructed from more fundamental systems.

    This means that the usual picture we have of mathematics:

    Numbers
Geometry
Algebra
Calculus
Logic
Sets
Probability
...

    as a collection of separate subjects is somewhat misleading.

    There are deep connections between them.

    You can sometimes take one system and build another system on top of it.

    For example, arithmetic can be developed from logical and set-theoretic foundations.

    And the connection goes in the other direction too: logic itself can be implemented using physical systems such as digital circuits.

    So we get a rather beautiful map of connections:

    flowchart TD
    L["Logic"] --> S["Sets"]
    S --> N["Numbers"]
    N --> A["Arithmetic"]
    L --> C["Computation"]
    C --> P["Programs"]
    L --> D["Digital circuits"]

    This shouldn’t be interpreted as a strict hierarchy where one subject is simply “more fundamental” than another.

    It is better thought of as a map of connections.

    The same ideas can be expressed in different languages.

    12. Why should you care?#

    At this point you might reasonably ask:

    “Okay, but why are we talking about this?”

    There are two reasons.

    First: logic is everywhere.#

    Whenever you have to decide whether something is true, or whether several conditions are simultaneously true, you are doing logic.

    Is it raining?
Is the shop open?
Do I have enough money?
Is the number even?

    And when you combine those questions:

    It is raining AND I have an umbrella.

The shop is open OR it is Saturday.

The number is NOT even.

    you are doing Boolean logic.

    Second: recognising the structure is more useful than memorising the rules.#

    You don’t want to become someone who has memorised a list of Boolean identities but doesn’t recognise when they are useful.

    You want to be able to look at something unfamiliar and think:

    “Hang on. This looks like AND and OR.”

    That is a much more powerful skill.

    13. And eventually, programming#

    You have only just started programming, so you don’t need to know how deeply logic connects to computer science yet.

    For now, just notice the obvious connection.

    The things you have been writing:

    if x > 10:
    ...

    and:

    if raining and carrying_umbrella:
    ...

    are logical statements.

    When your programs become more complicated, you will spend a surprising amount of time expressing conditions clearly:

    if age >= 18 and has_ticket:
    ...

    The programming language gives you a convenient syntax, but underneath it is still the same basic machinery:

    TRUE
FALSE
AND
OR
NOT

    The computer isn’t doing something fundamentally different from the logic we have been discussing.

    It is simply very, very good at it.

    14. Exercises#

    The best way to get comfortable with logic is to play with it.

    Exercise 1 — Everyday logic#

    Write each of these using AND, OR, and NOT.

    a.

    I will go for a walk if it is not raining.

    b.

    I will have dessert if I have finished dinner and there is some left.

    c.

    I will watch a movie if it is Friday or Saturday.

    Try giving names to the individual statements first.

    Exercise 2 — De Morgan’s theorem#

    Rewrite these without an outer NOT:

    NOT (It is raining AND it is cold)
    NOT (I have a ticket OR I have a pass)

    Say the resulting statements out loud. If they sound strange, that’s fine. The point is to convince yourself that the two versions mean the same thing.

    Exercise 3 — The four forms#

    Start with:

    If it is raining, then the ground is wet.

    Write down:

    1. The conditional
    2. The converse
    3. The inverse
    4. The contrapositive

    Then identify which ones are logically equivalent.

    Try the same exercise with:

    If a number is divisible by 10, then it is divisible by 5.

    Exercise 4 — Tea and coffee#

    Suppose:

    A = people who like tea
B = people who like coffee

    Explain in plain English what each of these means:

    A ∩ B
A ∪ B
Aᶜ

    Now invent your own two sets and do the same thing.

    Exercise 5 — Find logic around you#

    For one day, pay attention to statements containing words such as:

    and
or
not
if
unless
either
both

    Write down five examples.

    Then try translating each one into a logical expression.

    You will probably discover that people use logic constantly without ever calling it logic.

    The big idea#

    Don’t worry about memorising every symbol or terminology you encountered in this post.

    The important idea is much simpler:

    Logic gives us a language for talking precisely about what is true, what is false, and how the truth of one statement relates to another.

    And once you learn to recognise that structure, you start seeing it everywhere.

    In ordinary language:

    It is raining AND I have an umbrella.

    In sets:

    A ∩ B

    In programming:

    a and b

    In digital electronics:

    AND gate

    These are not four completely unrelated ideas.

    They are four different ways of expressing a remarkably similar idea.

    That is perhaps the most useful lesson to take away from logic:

    the symbols may change, the terminology may change, and even the subject may change—but the underlying structure can remain the same.

    Further Rabbit Holes#

    If this post has made you even slightly curious about logic, there are some wonderfully strange places you can go next.

    You don’t need to study these topics systematically. Pick one that sounds interesting and follow the rabbit hole.

    People worth knowing#

    George Boole#

    The obvious place to start.

    Boole developed the algebra of logic that we now call Boolean algebra—long before computers existed.

    If you want to understand where the AND, OR, and NOT you use in programming ultimately come from, start with George Boole.

    Lewis Carroll#

    Yes, that Lewis Carroll—the author of Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland.

    Carroll was also deeply interested in logic and wrote books and puzzles about it. His logical puzzles are a delightful reminder that formal reasoning doesn’t have to look like a mathematics textbook.

    Look for:

    • Lewis Carroll’s logic puzzles
    • Carroll’s syllogisms
    • Symbolic Logic

    It’s particularly amusing to discover that the person who wrote about the Mad Hatter and the Cheshire Cat was also spending his time thinking about formal reasoning.

    Bertrand Russell#

    Russell was one of the great logicians and philosophers of the early 20th century.

    He is particularly famous for Russell’s Paradox, which begins with a deceptively innocent question:

    What happens if we consider the set of all sets that do not contain themselves?

    That tiny question caused serious problems for attempts to build all of mathematics from set theory.

    A more accessible version is the Barber Paradox:

    A barber shaves everyone in the village who does not shave themselves. Does the barber shave himself?

    Try answering it before looking up the solution.

    Kurt Gödel#

    Gödel took the question of mathematical logic much further.

    His incompleteness theorems showed, roughly speaking, that sufficiently powerful formal mathematical systems contain statements that are true but cannot be proved using the rules of that system.

    That’s an astonishing result.

    If you enjoy questions like:

    “Can mathematics prove everything that is true?”

    then Kurt Gödel is your rabbit hole.

    Alan Turing#

    Turing connects logic directly to computing.

    Before modern computers existed, Turing described an extremely simple theoretical machine that could perform computations. These Turing machines became one of the foundations of theoretical computer science.

    Even more interesting is the halting problem:

    Can we write a program that looks at any other program and determines whether that program will eventually stop?

    The answer is no.

    This is a wonderful example of logic telling us something fundamental about computers—not merely how to build them, but what computers can and cannot do.

    Raymond Smullyan#

    If the historical figures sound a little intimidating, try Raymond Smullyan.

    Smullyan wrote numerous books turning formal logic into puzzles, games, riddles, and detective problems.

    A particularly famous family of puzzles is Knights and Knaves:

    • Knights always tell the truth.
    • Knaves always lie.
    • You meet some people.
    • They make statements.
    • You have to work out who is who.

    These puzzles are an excellent way to practice logical reasoning without feeling like you’re doing mathematics.

    Paradoxes worth exploring#

    Paradoxes are particularly good introductions to logic because they start with questions that sound completely reasonable and then lead somewhere very strange.

    The Liar Paradox#

    Consider:

    This statement is false.

    If the statement is true, then it is false.

    If it is false, then it is true.

    So… what is going on?

    This is one of the oldest and most famous examples of self-reference causing trouble.

    Russell’s Paradox#

    This is the mathematical cousin of the Liar Paradox.

    Very roughly:

    Consider the set of all sets that do not contain themselves.

    Now ask:

    Does that set contain itself?

    If it does, then by its definition it shouldn’t.

    If it doesn’t, then by its definition it should.

    The question seems almost silly. Its consequences were anything but silly.

    Search for Russell’s Paradox if you want to see how a seemingly harmless idea about sets helped shake the foundations of mathematics.

    Zeno’s Paradoxes#

    Zeno of Elea came up with several paradoxes involving motion.

    The most famous involves Achilles and the tortoise.

    Suppose a tortoise gets a head start in a race. Before Achilles can catch the tortoise, he must reach the point where the tortoise was. But by then the tortoise has moved a little further. Achilles reaches that point, but the tortoise has moved again.

    So does Achilles ever catch the tortoise?

    Of course he does.

    But explaining exactly why the argument appears to suggest otherwise leads into some fascinating ideas about infinity, limits, and calculus.

    The Monty Hall Problem#

    This one is especially fun because your intuition will probably tell you one thing while probability tells you another.

    You are shown three doors:

    Door 1    Door 2    Door 3
  🚗        🐐        🐐

    Behind one is a car. Behind the other two are goats.

    You choose a door.

    The host, who knows where the car is, opens one of the other doors and reveals a goat.

    You are then given a choice:

    Stay with your original door, or switch to the remaining unopened door?

    Should you switch?

    Don’t look up the answer immediately. Work it out first.

    Search for Monty Hall Problem when you’re ready to see why the answer is surprising.

    The Unexpected Hanging Paradox#

    A judge tells a prisoner:

    “You will be executed next week, but the execution will be a surprise.”

    The prisoner reasons about which day the execution can occur and apparently manages to conclude that it cannot happen at all.

    And yet, when the execution happens, it is still a surprise.

    This is a particularly nice example of reasoning about what someone knows about what someone else knows.

    Search for Unexpected Hanging Paradox or Surprise Examination Paradox.

    Logic puzzles#

    If you would rather do logic than read about it, look for:

    Knights and Knaves#

    As mentioned above, these are Raymond Smullyan’s classic truth-teller/liar puzzles.

    They are probably the closest thing to a logic workout disguised as a game.

    The Hardest Logic Puzzle Ever#

    This is a famous puzzle by George Boolos involving three gods:

    • one always tells the truth,
    • one always lies,
    • one answers randomly.

    You have to determine who is who.

    There are additional complications involving the fact that the gods speak a language in which you don’t know which word means “yes” and which means “no.”

    It is spectacularly over-engineered.

    It is also a wonderful demonstration of just how much information can be packed into carefully constructed questions.

    Logic puzzle books by Raymond Smullyan#

    If the Knights and Knaves puzzles appeal to you, Smullyan has plenty more.

    Look for:

    • What Is the Name of This Book?
    • The Riddle of Scheherazade
    • Alice in Puzzle-Land

    The last one is especially appropriate if the Lewis Carroll connection interests you.

    Logic and computers#

    There is a natural progression from the logic you’ve just learned to computer science.

    Start with logic gates.

    An AND gate is essentially:

    A AND B

    An OR gate is:

    A OR B

    A NOT gate is:

    NOT A

    Combine enough of these simple components and you can build increasingly complicated digital circuits.

    Eventually you can build things capable of performing arithmetic and computation.

    That gives you a beautiful connection:

    Logic
   ↓
Logic gates
   ↓
Digital circuits
   ↓
Computers

    Turing machines#

    Then look at Turing machines.

    Don’t be put off by the name. The basic idea is remarkably simple: imagine a machine that reads symbols from a tape, follows a small set of rules, and changes the symbols on the tape.

    That’s it.

    From this almost ridiculously simple setup, Turing was able to investigate what it means for something to be computable.

    The Halting Problem#

    Once you know a little programming, try this question:

    Can you write a program that can look at any program and tell you whether that program will eventually finish or run forever?

    It sounds like something a sufficiently clever programmer should be able to do.

    It turns out that no such general program can exist.

    This is the Halting Problem, and it is one of the great results connecting logic with computer science.

    It is worth thinking about before reading the proof.

    And finally: Gödel, Escher, Bach#

    If you want to go much further down the rabbit hole, there is Douglas Hofstadter’s Gödel, Escher, Bach.

    It brings together:

    • logic
    • mathematics
    • computation
    • music
    • art
    • recursion
    • self-reference
    • consciousness

    It is not an easy book, and you absolutely do not need to read it for this course.

    But it is a fascinating example of what happens when you start looking for the same patterns across completely different subjects.

    And that, ultimately, is what this entire post has been trying to get you to notice.

    If you don’t know where to begin, try this:

    George Boole

    You don’t need to follow either path to the end.

    Pick whichever question makes you curious enough to search for the answer. That’s usually the best way to learn.
